# E91 build plan: the price signal and the substation

Low Carbon London smart meters, a CPU fleet of household fits, and simulation-based calibration of
the whole pipeline. PyMC-first. Plan only: no notebook, no model has been fitted.

**Status: CONDITIONAL.** The data are usable and the assignment is now established, but it is not
what the original pitch assumed. Households were **not randomised to the tariff**. Event days were.
The notebook is rebuilt around that. Three pilot gates can still stop or shrink it: P1 (meter
clock), P2 (calendar conflict) and P5 (pre-trial placebo).

Number labels: **[M]** measured here today. **[P]** published by the trial team, not reproduced
here. **[X]** extrapolated, nothing behind it has run. **[A]** an assumption for the owner to set.
All [M] data figures come from a sample of 104 homes (partitions 0, 84, 167). The file is sorted by
group then id, so the sample is two runs of consecutive Std ids and the last ToU ids, not a random
draw. Treat it as a schema check and a preview, never as an estimate.

## 1. Trial assignment: established first

Sources read today in full text: Report A3 (Schofield et al. 2014, from the Wayback Machine; the
UKPN link is dead), Schofield's PhD thesis ch. 4-8, the UK Data Service user guide for SN 7857
(same trial, same authors), the CIRED 2015 paper and the Ofgem slide deck. Report A2 (attitudes,
incentive detail) was **not** read.

| Contrast | How it was assigned | Evidence | Use in E91 |
|---|---|---|---|
| dToU vs flat (D vs N) | **Two-stage opt-in, then eligibility filters.** EDF customers opted in to the project; a subset then opted in to the tariff. | Guide: "group N was not a true control". Thesis: "it was assumed that self selection had created a natural difference". Thesis: "should not be used directly as the baseline". | **Never an effect.** N is only a measure of shared day shocks. |
| Supply-following (SF) event days within D | **Randomised block.** 93 events sweep the clock; each type is repeated 3 times and "randomly distributed" over the year. | A3 sec. 3; thesis sec. 4.6-4.8, ch. 6 uses SF because of this. | **Primary causal contrast.** |
| Constraint-management (CM) event days within D | **Not randomised.** Placed on the viable days left after SF, aimed at seasonal peaks. Low-High-Low on one day. | Thesis sec. 4.7-4.8. | Secondary. Descriptive unless P4 and P5 pass. |
| Which households respond | Nothing assigned. | - | Descriptive. Targeting only by cross-fitting. |
| D before 2013 | No prices at all. | Thesis sec. 5.4.3. | Placebo. |

What this means:

1. **The estimand** is the change in a household's mean hourly load during an announced 2013 price
   event, against that same household on a default-price day, among households that accepted both
   opt-ins, passed the filters, and were paid to stay. It is not "the effect of offering a tariff".
2. **Filters** (thesis 4.4.2): no Economy 7, dual fuel, prepayment, micro-generation or account
   debt; working in-home display; survey returned; not planning to move within 6 months.
3. **Incentives** [P]: £100 to sign up, £50 to stay, £20 per survey, a prize draw, and a guarantee of
   reimbursement if worse off. A real scheme would differ, and the direction of the bias is unknown.
   The within-D contrast also misses any effect of being on the tariff at all (default 11.76p against
   the flat 14.228p), and it cannot separate a response from anticipation: notices went out at 08:30
   the day before [P], so hours before an event are checked rather than assumed clean.
4. **The randomisation is one shared calendar.** Every D home saw the same events. So the effective
   sample for a calendar-level effect is the **number of events**, not homes x events. In the file
   [M], 15 randomised SF High events touch 17:00-23:00, and **only 4 of those 15 fall in GMT months;
   11 are in BST**, when evening load is low. The 22 CM-type evening days are mostly winter, but not
   randomised (16 undisputed, 6 disputed). So for a winter evening peak, the case a substation
   cares about, the randomised evidence is **4 events**, and anything stronger leans on CM days or
   on carrying a summer effect to winter (the thesis finds response falls with temperature [P]).
   That count, not 1,025 homes, limits "will it deliver on the day". This is the notebook's lesson.
5. The randomisation mechanism (seed, how the weekly limit of 3 event days was enforced) is not
   published, so an exact randomisation test is not possible. Pilot P4 approximates it.
6. Was the calendar fixed before the year, rather than chosen using forecasts? Both reports describe
   a designed schedule, but neither says so outright. This is an inference. Preview balance check
   [M, 73 Std homes, 17:00-23:00, against no-event days of the same month and day type]: SF days
   -2.3 W (se 5.5), CM days -2.2 W (se 11.7). No sign of chasing cold days within a month, on a weak
   test. CM is seasonal: 22 of 25 CM-type days fall in Oct-Mar, against half of the year.
7. SF month and weekday counts look uniform [M]: chi-square p = 0.86 (month), 0.46 (weekday).

Corrections to the survey and skeptic notes: the "8% response rate" was not found in the deck or
reports I read (the thesis's 8% is a demand reduction, eq. 8.4); the slides say "assumes 24% uptake"
with no denominator I could trace, so treat acceptance as an owner assumption. "As close as possible
to a random trial" holds for event timing, not for households. E10, E14 and E68 already touch SBC.

## 2. Stakeholder brief and decision

*A network planner at a London distribution operator (a hypothetical role; the substation is
hypothetical too, because the public data carry no location). A substation needs either
reinforcement or a credible peak reduction.*

> We can reinforce, or we can pay for a price signal. Before I put a price signal in the plan I need
> a sentence I can defend: how many homes do we have to sign up to have a stated chance of taking a
> stated number of kilowatts off the evening peak, on the day we need it? Is it worth chasing the
> best responders, or do we enrol everyone? And how far can a 2013 London trial be trusted for that?

**Decision the notebook ends on:** a table of the probability of delivering a target cut, for a
sequence of enrolment sizes, under stated acceptance rates, plus a go, no-go or "run a local trial
first" recommendation. The honest default is the third unless the numbers are very clear.

Scale, from published numbers only [P, X]: about 50 W per home in CM peak hours and about 30 W in SF
events; after-diversity peak about 1 kW per home; about 8% of baseline. So 1 MW needs about 20,000
responding homes at 50 W, and about 83,000 offers at the slides' 24% uptake. The notebook must
replace this with its own posterior, and may well disagree.

Scope limits stated in the brief: 2013 London, volunteers, paid and bill-protected, before heat pumps
and EVs at scale, one network area, and a substation modelled as a random mix of homes.

## 5. Model

All PyMC, nutpie via one compiled model and `.with_data`. No JAX in the pool; only P12 imports it.
`OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1` is set before NumPy, one thread per worker, one compile per
worker unless pickling is proved (the survey's "one compile shared" is unconfirmed). Spawn, never fork.

**Ladder** (each rung shown, price stated, and whether it changed the decision):

**M0, naive.** D minus N during events. Shown to fail (section 6).

**M1, closed-form fleet.** Same design as M2 with Gaussian noise and conjugate priors, in seconds.
This is the published method's descendant and the strong simple baseline. Its output is the *mean*
effect, which is what adds up to kW at a substation.

**M2, household fleet** (about 61 parameters per home):

```
y[h,t]  mean kW in hour t
m[h,t] = b[h, s(t)] + beta[h] * z[t] + sum_k delta[h,k] * X[k,t]
y[h,t] ~ StudentT(nu[h], m[h,t], sigma[h])
```

- `s(t)`: 24 hours x weekday/weekend, 48 levels.
- `z[t]`: standardised mean N load at hour t, the shared-shock measure (the published baseline's
  idea, coarser than its 337 per-home coefficients).
- **Placebo circularity.** A fleet of N homes regressed on the N mean has a mean effect near 0 by
  construction, so it could only test spread. Split N at random by id hash: half A builds `z` for
  placebo fits on half B. D homes may use all of N.
- `X`: SF High x 4 time bands, SF Low x 4 bands, CM High, CM Low. 10 effects. Hours next to events
  stay in, through these terms, so that shifting load is measured rather than deleted.
- Priors in kW: `b ~ Normal(0.4, 0.5)`, `beta ~ Normal(1, 1)`, `delta ~ Normal(0, 0.3)`,
  `sigma ~ LogNormal(log 0.3, 0.7)`, `nu = 2 + Gamma(2, 0.1)` so the mean exists. Prior predictive
  on real scale before any fit.
- Window: July 2012 to February 2014. Disputed days get their own term until P2 decides.
- **A Student-t location is not a mean.** Load residuals are skewed and showers and ovens live in
  the tail, so the robust fit can move the effect the substation does not care about, or hide the
  effect it does. Hence P10.

**M3, pooling (the shortcut under test).** `delta[h,k] ~ Normal(mu[k], tau[k])`, with two versions:
- **M3a, Gaussian plug-in:** each home enters as `Normal(mean, sd)` of its stage-1 draws.
- **M3b, stored draws:** `pm.Potential` of the per-home log-mean over 400 stored draws of
  `Normal(draw | mu, tau)`, divided by the stage-1 prior density. About 400k terms per gradient.
Likely covariate: pre-trial evening share of load only, because the release has nothing else.

**M4, crossed event model.** The new part, and the reason section 1 matters. Mean residual `r[h,e]` of
home h in event e, in High hours touching 17:00-23:00:

```
r[h,e] ~ StudentT(nu, mu[c(e)] + u[h] + v[e], sigma)
u[h] ~ Normal(0, tau_h)     # households differ
v[e] ~ Normal(0, tau_e)     # days differ, and every home shares that
```

About 37 evening events x ~1,050 homes = about 39,000 cells, 1,090 parameters. `c(e)` separates SF
and CM, each by GMT or BST season: that gives SF-winter 4 events, SF-summer 11, CM mostly winter. SF
vs CM cannot be split from low-price flanks or non-random timing, so the SF-CM gap is reported,
not explained. Check `pm.ZeroSumNormal` on `u`; judge on `mu` and `tau`, not on `u`. Report `tau_e`
with a wide-prior sensitivity check: these counts identify it weakly, and that weakness is a finding.

**M5, joint check.** One PyMC model for 100 homes (M2 + M3 at once) over three months, to test the
two-stage shortcut against what it approximates. Cut to 50 homes if over 30 min.

**Delivery.** For enrolment N, acceptance a, target Y: total cut on one event day is `N a (mu + v)`
plus household noise of order `sqrt(N a) * sd`. From the draws of M4, P(total >= Y). A second curve
assumes independence (no `v`) to show the difference. Enrolment shrinks only the second term. No
number of homes averages out a shared bad day, it can only buy margin.

**Targeting.** Each SF type has 3 repeats. Rank homes on two repeats, measure on the third (cross-fit).
The published "best 25% give three times the mean" [P] ranks on the same events it measures, so
expect shrinkage. The DNO has only past load, so also try ranking on pre-trial shape alone.

**Predictive check.** On held-out repeats, never on data used for the fit, and on the group total as
well as households. No reuse of fitted home effects from the same event.

real N homes, add a known response on D's calendar, run the pipeline, and report recovery. Real
noise, known truth, and no prices. 100 replicates, 25 homes.

## 7. CPU against GPU

The fleet is CPU work because each model is small and E86 found no GPU gain on small models.
ChEES pools across chains of one target, which means nothing across different homes, so MEADS and
ChEES do not apply and the notebook says so. Compare against the best CPU setup, one thread per
worker. Skeptic point 4: X is mostly shared, so a batched solve may beat the 0.3 ns per element
guess, so **measure 200 homes before any claim** (P12). Return something depending on positions,
or XLA deletes the sampler; `float32` only after a precision check; `jax_enable_x64` set explicitly.

## 9. Checkpoint stages

Order for `store.next_stage`: `fetch`, `calendar`, `parse`, `assemble`, `pilot_clock`,
`pilot_calendar`, `pilot_balance`, `gauss_fleet`, `pilot_benchmark`, `robust_fleet`, `pooled`,
`crossed`, `joint`, `sbc`, `plasmode`, `gpu_compare`, `delivery`, `figures`.

- Chunks: `readings` (one per member), `fleet_d`, `fleet_n` (10 homes each, about 557 chunks), `sbc`
  (one per replicate), `plasmode`.
- Every write goes through `bigrun`: temp file, fsync, replace, checksum. A pool shares nothing
  except `Store`; `pending` re-hashes, so call it once at start rather than per worker.

## 11. Communication lesson

1. **Say what was and was not randomised, in the first paragraph, in plain words.** "Nobody tossed a
   coin for who got the tariff. Somebody did toss a coin for which days had price events." Every
   later claim is then labelled by which of the two it rests on.
2. **Procurement language.** Not "the mean effect is X W" but "sign N homes for a P% chance of Y kW on
   the day, if a of those offered accept". Acceptance is shown as an input the planner owns.
3. **Report validation to a non-statistician.** "We ran the whole analysis 250 times on made-up homes
   where we knew the answer, and again on real homes with a response we added ourselves. Here is how
   often it was right." Then what that test cannot see.
4. **Name the unit that limits you.** A thousand homes, and four randomised winter evenings: be
   plain that the four set the limit on "will it work on the day".
5. **Price the analysis.** Open with what the closed-form answer says in seconds, and state at the end
   whether the expensive run changed the decision.
