## Stakeholder brief and decision

The stakeholder is hypothetical and the notebook says so.

> I coordinate float deployments for a national Argo programme. I can place 20 floats in the North
> Atlantic in the coming year. Where should they go to reduce our uncertainty about upper-ocean
> temperature anomalies the most, and how much of that advice would change if your model is wrong?
> A float drifts, so do not give me fixed stations. I also need to know whether your recommendation
> beats simply filling the biggest gaps.

**Decision:** an ordered list of 20 launch sites, or the finding that the choice barely matters.
The notebook ends in one of three recommendations, stated in advance:

1. **Target these sites**: the plan beats gap-filling, and the sites agree across models.
2. **Fill the gaps**: the simple rule does about as well. Skip the big model for this decision.
3. **Target a region, not sites**: models agree on a region and disagree on sites.

Context (measured): 84 floats were at cycle 0 or 1 in the box in 2025, 78 in 2024 and 45 in 2023, so
20 is about a quarter of a recent year. 454 floats reported from the box in 2025.

The objective is the mean posterior variance of the 100 dbar temperature anomaly over the target
region and year. Two limits go in the brief: this scores one level of a float that samples 2,000 m,
and it ignores ship availability.

## Variance siting here, value siting in E89

Both notebooks ask "where should the next one go?", and they answer with different objectives. E89's
main failure is "uncertainty is not value". E92 ranks sites by uncertainty. That needs a stated reason.

| | E92: floats | E89: monitors |
|---|---|---|
| What the answer is for | A better map of the 100 dbar anomaly | A correct call on each county against a legal line |
| Score | Mean posterior variance over the target region | Population-weighted expected wrong calls, `min(p, 1 - p)` |
| Loss assumed | Squared error, equal over the target | Right or wrong at a threshold |
| A site is worth something when | It reduces map error anywhere in the target | A reading could change a call |
| Observed values | Do not enter at fixed hyperparameters: the ranking is geometry | Enter through `mu_t` and `p_t`: a certain county is worth nothing |

**The difference is the loss.** If the map is the product and the loss is squared error, the best
report is the posterior mean, its expected loss is the posterior variance, and the expected value of a
new float is the variance it removes. So variance is the value of information here, but only under
that loss. E89 has a threshold, so uncertainty far from the line buys nothing. The two plans do not
contradict each other, because they assume different losses.

**The weak point is that this loss is an assumption.** The stakeholder is hypothetical and the brief
was written to ask for variance. No real decision downstream has been named. If a real coordinator
needs a specific product, such as a forecast or a heat-content index for one region, E89's argument
applies and this ranking may be the wrong target.

**What the notebook must do:**

- Say "removes the most map uncertainty", never "most valuable", and point to E89's F2.
- State the squared-error assumption beside the recommendation, on the closing card.
- Rerun the greedy pick with the target weight moved to the Gulf Stream box, and report how many of
  the top 20 change. Greedy costs about 1.3e10 flops (extrapolated), so this is cheap. It tests the
  equal weighting and does not test a threshold loss.

## The model

**Anomaly.** y_i = T100_i - climatology(place, day of year). Climatology comes from 2015-2022 only, by
local weighted regression with a local quadratic and three annual harmonics, as in Kuusela and Stein
(2018), so no fitted or scored row enters its own mean. The bandwidth is adaptive (distance to the
400th nearest base profile, clipped to 150-1,000 km), because a fixed 1,000 km window left up to 6 C
of mean error along the Gulf Stream (see "Pilot results, 2026-11-03").

**Racers**, all scored on the same held-out floats:

| | Model | Cost driver |
|---|---|---|
| A | Exact stationary GP over the basin | n^3, dense, hand-coded gradient |
| B | Moving-window local GP: about 80 windows, each with its own hyperparameters | Many small exact fits |
| C | Vecchia GP (m = 30), same kernel as A | n batched solves of size m + 1 |
| A' | The fix: exact with amplitude map, K = D R D + noise, D from B | As A |

**Kernel for A, C and A'.** Mesoscale term plus basin-scale term plus nugget:

    k = s1^2 M(r1) + s2^2 M(r2) + sn^2 delta
    r1^2 = ((dx - c dt)/lx)^2 + (dy/ly)^2 + (dt/lt)^2

- About 10 hyperparameters: two amplitudes, two sets of lengthscales, the westward propagation speed
  c, and the nugget. All are sampled on the log scale with LogNormal priors, except c, which gets a
  Normal. E86's half-normal tail problem is why.
- M is Matern 1/2 or 3/2, decided at P6. Kuusela and Stein use the exponential. ExpQuad is used only
  to show the numerical failure.
- A marginalised intercept and linear trend stay in the model, because 2023-2025 are not centred on
  the base period.
- Coordinates are decided at P6: equirectangular about 37.5N, or a Mercator warp. Under the warp a
  stationary kernel has physical scales shrinking with cos(latitude), which is the right direction
  physically. Both are fixed maps, so the kernel remains positive definite.

**Why not HSGP.** With a 2-year span over 7,000 x 3,900 km, the rule of thumb gives about 375,000 basis
functions for Matern 3/2 and about 50,000 for ExpQuad at c = 1.5 and lengthscales 300 km, 200 km, 15
days (extrapolated, and I recalled the rule's constants from Riutort-Mayol et al., so verify them). A
24k x 375k float32 basis is 36 GB, and M exceeds n, so the basis saves nothing. This also honours the
E86 boundary lesson: c = 1.25 was too small there. Scattered floats give no Kronecker shortcut.

**Hyperparameter uncertainty (A).** Mode by L-BFGS, Hessian by central differences of the gradient,
Gaussian approximation, 64 Sobol points reweighted with PSIS, then 10 draws for the design. This
replaces the survey's central-composite design: PSIS gives a diagnostic and the cost is one fixed
number. Racers B and C get plug-in hyperparameters, and the prose says so.

**PyMC cross-check.** Plain JAX does the large fits. `pm.gp.Marginal`, or `wrap_jax` around the same
function, samples the hyperparameters under nutpie at n = 400 (SMALL), and n = 800 and 2,000 (stored
runs). This audits both the Laplace step and the hand-coded log density.

The cost is 21,000 gradients a chain: 1,400 steps x 15 leapfrogs (both assumed). Two per-gradient
rates exist, and the second is the one to expect, because this plan pins BLAS to one thread for nutpie:

| n | E62's 168 ms at n = 2,000, threads unknown | 384 ms, Cholesky plus K^-1 on one thread (critic, measured) | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400 | 2.2 ms, 46 s | 5.0 ms, 105 s | extrapolated, n^2.7, far below the anchors |
| 800 | 14 ms, 4.9 min | 32 ms, 11 min (critic's figure: about 13 min) | extrapolated, n^2.7 |
| 2,000 | 168 ms, 59 min | 384 ms, 2.2 h | arithmetic on one rate each |

These are wall times for one chain, and 4 chains in parallel take the same only with 4 free cores.
The earlier rows here said 30-60 s and 6-10 min: both were wrong by about ten times, as 1,400 x 15 x
14 ms is 294 s and 1,400 x 15 x 168 ms is 59 min. Neither rate includes kernel construction or
PyTensor overhead, so these are lower bounds. The n = 800 run no longer fits SMALL.

**Implementation rules from the audit and E86:**

- Never broadcast `X[:, None] - X[None]`: at n = 30,000 that is 10.8 GB in float32. Build kernels in
  blocks of rows.
- Hand-code the gradient: `0.5 * tr((a a^T - K^-1) dK)` for each hyperparameter, and sum the trace over
  blocks so that no dK is held. Reverse-mode autodiff keeps several n x n intermediates.
- Sum the log diagonal in float64, whatever the factor's precision.
- Every timed function returns something that depends on the factor, or XLA deletes the work.
- Set `jax_enable_x64` explicitly. Import blackjax nowhere: E92 does not need it, and it starts CUDA.
- Detect a GPU with `bigrun.gpu_present()`.
- Set `OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1` before NumPy for nutpie. Set 15 threads for the CPU Cholesky baseline,
  in a separate process, if Q1 allows it: that baseline must be well configured, not crippled.
- Predictive checks use held-out floats. They never reuse a latent field fitted to the same data.
- Holdout is by whole float (leave-one-float-out). Kuusela and Stein show why: leaving out single
  profiles lets the same float's neighbours fill the gap.

## Failure and fix

### Evidence so far

All **measured** on 2,710 real 2023 profile locations in the Gulf Stream box, one year, one core, with
illustrative kernels and nothing fitted. Ratio means total variance over nugget variance.

| Kernel | Ratio | Condition no. | float32 Cholesky | log det error | objective error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matern 1/2, 300 km, 200 km, 15 d | 10 to 1e6 | up to 1.4e3 | ok | under 0.001 | under 0.001 |
| Matern 3/2, same | 10 to 1e6 | up to 1.7e5 | ok | up to 0.002 | up to 0.003 |
| Two-scale Matern 3/2 | 10 to 1e6 | up to 6.3e6 | ok | up to 0.007 | up to 0.005 |
| ExpQuad, same short scales | 1e6 | 3.8e7 | ok | **-3.9** | -0.05 |
| ExpQuad 1,500 km, 200 d | 1e3 | 1.7e6 | ok | 0.018 | -0.21 |
| ExpQuad 1,500 km, 200 d | 1e4 | 1.7e7 | ok, **silently wrong** | **-15.8** | **2.1** |
| ExpQuad 1,500 km, 200 d | 1e5 and 1e6 | 1.7e8 and 1.7e9 | **fails** | | |

Also measured: 50 exact duplicate locations under Matern 3/2 failed only at ratio 1e7. Vecchia on a
draw from a two-scale Matern 3/2 at the true kernel: m = 30 was 3-7 nats below exact with maxmin
ordering and 6-10 with time ordering, or 0.001-0.004 per observation. m = 15 was 16-19 below, m = 5 was
56-84 below. Extending the first to 24,000 points gives roughly 30-90 nats (extrapolated).

These tests cannot settle n = 30,000. Basin-wide data raise lambda_max, and the GPU's solver is not
OpenBLAS. Hence P9.

### Failure 1, the expected headline: exact but wrong

The stationary exact GP gets the most expensive computation in the notebook and its uncertainty
is still wrong where it matters. **Expected, not yet seen.** Kuusela and Stein found nonstationarity
and heavy tails crucial for calibration, and the skeptics pointed at the Gulf Stream.

- **Symptom:** at nominal 90%, whole-float coverage inside the Gulf Stream box is below 80% and
  above nominal in the gyre, while basin-wide coverage looks acceptable. A single coverage number
  hides it. This is the failure of checking at the wrong level, already noted in E77.
- **Consequence for the decision:** with fixed hyperparameters the posterior variance does not depend
  on observed values, so a stationary model's ranking is pure geometry. It sends floats to data voids
  regardless of where the ocean varies. That is the deeper version of the failure.
- **Fix:** A', an amplitude map from the moving-window variances, so K = D R D + noise. It stays exact.
  If tails remain, add a Student-t nugget through scale-mixture weights, one Cholesky per iteration.
  Target for A': coverage of at least 85% in the box.

### Failure 2, narrower: float32 can be silently wrong

Show only what is measured. At real float density, Matern kernels were fine. A smooth long-scale
ExpQuad with a small nugget gave no error and a log determinant 16 nats wrong, one step from a hard
failure. Precision is a modelling decision through the choice of kernel.

- **Fix:** Matern kernels, a nugget, the log determinant summed in float64, and a float64 audit.
- **Not the fix:** iterative refinement. It improves K^-1 y and does nothing for the log determinant,
  as the hardware skeptic noted. Mention it only for that reason.
- This contrasts with E86, where float32 error was benign for a different reason.

### Failure 3, smaller: fixed sites

Rank sites as if each float stays put, then with bootstrapped real tracks. Report how much of the top
20 changes.

**Pre-registered rule:** A beats a rival only if the 90% interval of the paired whole-float bootstrap of
the CRPS difference excludes zero, over all held-out floats and inside the box. Write this in before
looking at the scores.

**Validation of the decision itself.** A natural experiment: fit to the end of 2024 and map 2025 with and
without the floats launched in 2025 (84 in the box, measured count; the exact set is fixed at P2), then
score at held-out floats. Does predicted variance reduction match realised squared-error reduction?
This tests the objective. It cannot test sites nobody used: recommended sites get predicted values
only, and the notebook says so. Using 2025 positions of existing floats as "the network" is a mild
peek at the future, and the prose says that too.

## Communication lesson

**Say what the big model changed.** The notebook opens with a price table and the smallest model that
could answer the question, and closes by stating whether the expensive one moved the decision.
Three habits to teach:

1. Separate two questions a planner will merge: how sure are we about the map, and how sure are we
   about the advice? A narrow posterior can sit under advice that changes with the model.
2. Report the failure at the level of the decision. "90% coverage overall" is true and useless in the
   one current that matters.
3. Label every number. A measured dot and an extrapolated line are different claims, and the chart
   should make that visible.

The closing plain-language card states the recommendation, the evidence, what would change it, and
what was not tested.
